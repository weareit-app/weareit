**The key rule:** answer each question only from the relationships, facts, and outcomes the author actually wrote. Don't repair an incomplete graph with your own common sense. If the graph doesn't support an answer either way, record **"not supported by the graph"**; that is a finding, not a failure on your part.

## Part 1: questions about the situation as written

For each author, answer each question with:
- the conclusion the graph supports: **yes**, **no**, **not supported by the graph**, or **graph contradicts itself**;
- the specific lines that support it;
- **justified** or **unjustified**, judged against the notes.

1. Does the manager's "I'll think about it" establish a changed schedule?
2. Does Tuesday care with Mom cover the other three workdays?
3. If Mom stops helping, what goes away, and what stays?
4. Does being eligible for a subsidy establish that funding is available, or that a place has opened up?
5. Does joining the babysitting swap establish that its hours fit early shifts?
6. Does finding another family establish a nanny share, or is agreeing on the rules still needed?
7. Does any completed step say that "stop worrying about it every week" is reached?
8. **(Positive)** Does the graph identify at least one justified next thing to find out or try, and what is it?
9. **(Positive)** Does the graph keep Tuesday care as real help while recognizing that other days remain uncovered?

## Part 2: changes the authors didn't know about

Apply each change to each graph, using only what the graph says, and report what changes and what stays. Say whether the graph handles the change correctly, handles it incompletely, can't represent it, or gets it wrong.

- **Change A:** Mom stops helping on Tuesdays.
- **Change B:** The manager approves a later start on only one of the two days.
- **Change C:** Subsidy eligibility is confirmed, but whether funding is available is still unknown.

**Positive check:** after Change B, does the graph update the approach that depended on the later start (the cheaper place that opens at eight)? Is it now partly possible, still blocked, or not representable?

## Part 3: authoring quality

For each author, report:
- **Unsupported conclusions:** places the graph would support something the notes don't justify.
- **Omissions:** things in the notes the graph doesn't capture that affect conclusions.
- **Misused relationships:** for example, a "needs to succeed" that isn't actually necessary, a checklist that isn't one, or a missing withdrawal.
- **Honesty markers:** whether stated, assumed, and needs checking were used accurately.
- **Burden:** from the author's own Effort section, plus your read of the draft. What was hard, and what in the guide was ambiguous?

## Part 4: across authors

- Where did the authors **agree in conclusions**, even with different labels?
- Where did they **disagree**? Classify each disagreement as one of: ambiguous semantics in the guide, missing knowledge, a legitimate alternative, an authoring error, or unresolved. Don't force a disagreement into a category; "unresolved" is a valid answer.
- **Guide fixes:** specific wording changes that would likely have prevented the disagreements you found.

Be concrete and quote the drafts sparingly. End with a five-line summary: overall correctness, the biggest risk, the biggest guide fix, the authoring burden, and whether a second round looks worthwhile.
