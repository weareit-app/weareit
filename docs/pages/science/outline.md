# /science: How does It decide what to believe?

**Writer:** drafted from this outline; founder rewrites and approves. **Target:** about 400–500 words as the opening section of `/research` (see `questions.md` Q1). The atlas suggests `/science` "could become an accessible entry into /research rather than a competing catalog"; this outline takes that suggestion.

## 1. The question
- Most of what It shows (a fix that worked somewhere, a figure on `/risks`, a proof) is a claim someone could get wrong. How does a small project keep claims checkable, so a reader doesn't have to trust it?

## 2. What is known
- A claim is scientific when some observation could show it false (Popper). Say this in a sentence, and note that working scientists and philosophers dispute how far it goes.
- Many published findings don't hold up when repeated: the large psychology replication project, and the argument for why that's expected (Open Science Collaboration; Ioannidis).
- Machine-checked proofs move trust from a reader to a checker and to the stated assumptions (link the `/math` note when it exists).

## 3. Our present view
- Label: **planned practice, partly in use.** The rules It already follows, each one a link:
  - status labels on every result, view or plan (the public-essays spec);
  - sources next to the claims they support, and the essay's source list;
  - fix examples stated as what happened, with who, where and when (copy style guide);
  - reading research with verbatim quotes checked against the paper text (**You:** whether the private reading pipeline can be described).
- **You:** the rule you'd add that isn't written down yet.

## 4. Why it matters
- One example: a fix example on a pain page, traced to its source, with its status label and its one limit.

## 5. What remains open
- One founder reviews almost everything; that's a real limit on checking. No outside review yet.
- Status labels are assigned by the writer, so they can be wrong too.
