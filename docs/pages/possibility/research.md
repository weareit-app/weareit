# Possibility essay: research notes

Purpose: the evidence base for the public `/possibility` essay ("What does better look like, and what could make it reachable?").

Researched: 2026-10-20. Bracketed numbers refer to the sources list at the end. Researchers are described only by what they published. None of them is associated with It.

## Summary for the founder

- **Replicated finding** (five studies, all by one research group; the founder confirms this label, `questions.md` Q13). When the options are not obvious, people list fewer than half of their own relevant alternatives, and the ones they miss are about as good as the ones they list. Prompting people with their objectives raises both the number and the quality of the alternatives they come up with [1]. A related study found people leave out nearly half of their own objectives [2].
- **Replicated finding.** In organizations, decisions that impose one solution and limit the search for alternatives fail more often [4][5][6].
- **Working hypothesis.** The essay's central claim, that different goals and situations open more futures than people first see, fits the findings above. No one has tested it for life futures or for the public goods that It covers.
- **Established result.** Unemployment harms mental health, and the harm is partly causal [12][13]. Work also provides things other than income, such as time structure, status and collective purpose [14]. Life satisfaction often does not fully recover after reemployment [15][16].
- **Open question.** Whether unconditional cash "works" depends on the outcome you look at. Across the trials reviewed here, employment effects were small: slightly positive in Finland, slightly negative in the US. Wellbeing gains appeared, but some faded. No trial has tested a tax-financed, universal, permanent program in a rich country [18]–[23].
- **Open question.** The capability approach is the closest philosophical match for "dreams" and "fixes". Its main debates are still unsettled: whether to use a fixed list of capabilities, and how to measure them [7][10][11].
- **Suggestion for the essay** (a plan, not a finding). The unemployment pain could show several futures side by side: income without a job, a guaranteed job, stronger insurance, and earnings top-ups. Each one answers a different part of the loss.

## 1. More futures than people first see

**Value-focused thinking.** In his 1992 book [3], and in a 1996 article whose title names "identifying decision opportunities and creating alternatives" [3b], Keeney argues that decision makers should start from what they value rather than from the options already in front of them. Note: I checked the book's record but did not read the book. Its summary here comes from how Keeney's later papers describe it.

**Siebert and Keeney (2015) [1].** Published in *Operations Research* 63(5), the paper reports five empirical studies of important personal decisions. In the first study, decision makers identified "less than half" of their alternatives, and the alternatives they overlooked were of the same average quality as the ones they found. The other four studies found that prompting with objectives increased both the number and the quality of alternatives, with high statistical significance.

**Bond, Carlson and Keeney (2008) [2].** Across three studies, participants left out "nearly half" of the objectives that they later recognized as personally relevant. They rated the omitted objectives as almost as important as the ones they had listed. A case study of a high-tech firm repeated the pattern. This matters for It: people may miss not only ways forward but also parts of what they want.

**Nutt.** Nutt (1993) studied 168 strategic decision cases [4]. The approach of imposing "a fully developed solution" was widely used and seldom successful. Searching for multiple alternatives helped design-led approaches succeed. Nutt (1999) studied 358 decisions in US and Canadian organizations [5]. He found that about half failed, and he traced the failures to managers who imposed solutions and limited the search. Tactics prone to failure were used in two out of three decisions. Nutt (2004) recommends three ways to widen the search: identify stakeholders, set clearer objectives with the broadest scope, and use multiple perspectives [6]. A widely repeated statistic, that only a small share of decisions considered more than one alternative, is not verified. I could open the abstracts only, not the full texts.

**Narrow framing.** Kahneman and Lovallo (1993) describe a strong tendency to treat each choice as unique and to isolate it from future opportunities and past statistics [8]. Their paper is about risk and forecasting, not option generation. Use it only for the general point that frames narrow what people consider.

**Limits.** These studies cover individual and organizational decisions, measured with lab tasks, surveys and case reviews. None of them shows that a person's life gets better because they saw more options. "Goals before problems" is supported for generating options, but not established for outcomes.

## 2. The capability approach

**Core terms.** In "Equality of What?", delivered at Stanford on May 22, 1979 [9], Sen rejects utilitarian equality and Rawlsian primary goods as measures of advantage. He calls the focus on goods rather than on what they do for people a kind of "fetishism", and proposes "basic capability equality": what a person is actually able to do. The Stanford Encyclopedia entry by Robeyns and Byskov sets out the vocabulary used since then [10]. **Functionings** are "doings and beings". **Capabilities** are the real opportunity to achieve them. Sen also separates well-being freedom from **agency freedom**, which is the freedom to pursue goals that may not raise one's own well-being (Sen 1985, Dewey Lectures, as cited in [10]). The books *Commodities and Capabilities* (1985) and *Development as Freedom* (1999) are cited from the [10] bibliography; I did not open them.

**Adaptive preferences.** People in deprivation may lower their expectations as a way of coping ([10], citing Kynch and Sen 1983). Nussbaum (2001) argues that some existing preferences are bad bases for social policy. She notes that her approach, like Sen's, sets capability rather than actual functioning as the political goal, to avoid paternalism [11]. Her 2000 book develops the argument from the situation of poor women in India [11b].

**Nussbaum's list.** The list has ten central capabilities: life; bodily health; bodily integrity; senses, imagination and thought; emotions; practical reason; affiliation; other species; play; and control over one's environment ([10]; the list appears in *Creating Capabilities*, 2011 [11c]).

**Critiques.** Sen rejects a single fixed "canonical list". Critics argue that Nussbaum's list leaves too little room for democratic deliberation and agency [10]. Measurement is hard: it is "almost always easier" to observe functionings than capabilities (Sen 1992, quoted in [10]). Robeyns' open-access book is the standard survey of these disputes [7].

**Relevance to It.** A dream is close to a functioning a person values. A fix is close to something that widens real options, that is, capability. A person's first answer may reflect what they have learned to expect, which is the adaptive-preference worry. That is a reason to show several dreams, not to correct people's choices. The capability approach is a framework with open disputes, not a formal model of possibility. The essay should borrow its vocabulary only loosely.

## 3. Unemployment and wellbeing

- McKee-Ryan et al. (2005) analyzed 104 studies with 437 effect sizes [12]. Unemployed people had lower psychological and physical well-being than employed people. Coping resources, work-role centrality and appraisals were more strongly related to mental health than demographics were. The national level of unemployment benefits did not moderate the relationship.
- Paul and Moser (2009) analyzed 237 cross-sectional and 87 longitudinal studies [13]. The average effect was d = 0.51. Psychological problems affected 34% of unemployed people and 16% of employed people. Longitudinal studies and natural experiments support a causal reading. The effect was stronger where unemployment protection was weaker. Interventions for people who stayed unemployed were moderately effective (d = −0.35).
- **Jahoda's latent deprivation model.** Jahoda (1981 article, 1982 book) argued that employment provides income (the "manifest" function) and five "latent" functions: time structure, social contact, collective purpose, status and activity [14]. I could not open the 1981 article. The description here comes from Paul et al. (2023), a meta-analysis of 106 studies that tested the model [14b]. Employed people reported more of every function, with the largest gap for status (d = 0.94). The functions together explained about 19% of the variance in mental health. Income remained a significant predictor, which goes against Jahoda's view that financial loss is minor in welfare states.
- **Scarring.** Clark, Georgellis and Sanfey (2001) used 11 waves of German panel data [15]. Life satisfaction was lower for people with more past unemployment, not only for those currently unemployed. Lucas et al. (2004) followed more than 24,000 people in Germany over 15 years [16]. On average, life satisfaction did not fully return to its earlier level, even after reemployment.

**Implication.** "I can live well regardless of employment" has to answer two losses: income, and the non-income losses (structure, contact, purpose, status). Cash addresses the first directly. Cash addresses the second only indirectly, if at all.

## 4. Unconditional cash and UBI: a contested proposal

| Study | Design | Main findings | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Finland 2017–18** [17][17b] | 2,000 people aged 25–58 who were receiving basic unemployment benefits were randomly selected; €560 a month, tax free, for 2 years; participation was compulsory. | Employment rose by 6 days over Nov 2017–Oct 2018 ("modest"), more among foreign-language speakers and families with children. Recipients reported higher life satisfaction and less mental strain. | Only unemployed people were included. A 2018 "activation model" reform affected the groups unevenly. Survey response was 31% in the treatment group and 20% in the control group. One national design, so no community effects could be measured. Only one model was tested, so unconditionality cannot be separated from incentives. |
| **OpenResearch (Illinois, Texas)** [18][19] | 1,000 low-income adults got $1,000 a month for 3 years; 2,000 controls got $50 a month (Nov 2020–Oct 2023). | Labor market participation fell by 4.2 percentage points, and hours fell by 1–2 per week, with similar cuts by partners. No effect on job quality. Subjective well-being rose in year 1 and then returned to control levels. Stress and food security improved briefly. No physical-health gains. | Not universal, temporary, no financing tested. Figures changed across revisions. The current NBER revision is the one used here; a 2026 *QJE* publication is reported but not verified [18]. The often-quoted "1.3 hours" comes from an earlier version I did not open. |
| **Stockton SEED** [20] | 125 recipients got $500 a month for 24 months; 200 controls. Reported here: the pre-COVID first year (Feb 2019–Feb 2020). | Full-time employment rose from 28% to 40% among recipients, versus 32% to 37% among controls. Less income volatility, depression and anxiety. | Very small sample. A preliminary report, not peer reviewed. The authors say the findings "cannot point to larger labor market trends". |
| **GiveDirectly Kenya** [21] | 295 villages: control (100), 12-year UBI (44; US$0.75 per adult per day), 2-year UBI (80), lump sum of about US$500 (71). Surveys from Oct 2019 to Jan 2020. | More enterprises, and net revenues up a little over 50%. No change in total labor supply, but a shift from wage work to self-employment. Consumption up 5%. Better well-being and less depression. Lump sums had similar or larger economic effects. | Short-term results from a 2023 working paper, in a low-income rural setting. I found no later long-term paper. Two authors serve without pay as GiveDirectly directors (disclosed in the paper). |

**Critiques and alternatives.** Hoynes and Rothstein (2019) [22] estimate that $12,000 a year for each US adult would cost about $3 trillion a year, roughly 75% of 2017 federal spending. They find that a UBI would send much more money to middle-income households than existing programs do. Replacing current anti-poverty programs with a UBI would be "highly regressive" without substantial new revenue. They also argue that pilots will shed "little or no light" on long-term, general-equilibrium or universality effects. The 1970s negative income tax experiments found small labor-supply responses [22]. The essay can present several futures for the unemployment pain:

- **Income without a job:** UBI, or a negative income tax [22].
- **A job guaranteed:** an Austrian guaranteed-job pilot in Marienthal improved economic and non-economic well-being, including social recognition and time structure. The program sharply reduced long-term unemployment, with no negative employment spillovers [23]. This was one small town.
- **Earnings top-ups such as the EITC, and stronger unemployment insurance:** these are named here only as options. I did not research evidence for them in this pass.
- **Personal paths:** retraining, care or volunteer roles, and community roles that supply structure and purpose ([14b], [17b] on voluntary work).

## What the essay can safely say

- People often see fewer options and goals than they would recognize as their own, and starting from what they value helps [1][2].
- Losing work costs more than income [12]–[16].
- Unconditional cash trials found small employment effects in both directions and some wellbeing gains, some of them lasting and some short-lived. Serious researchers disagree about cost and design [17]–[22].
- UBI is one proposed future among several, alongside a job guarantee, a negative income tax, and stronger insurance.

## What it must not say

- That UBI works, or that it fails, overall. That any pilot tested a real UBI. That Finland "proved" anything about employment.
- That possibility is a formalized or measured quantity, or that It operationalizes the capability approach.
- That seeing more options improves lives. This has not been tested.
- That any cited researcher supports It or a particular policy.
