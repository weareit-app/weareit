# /possibility: What does better look like, and what could make it reachable?

**Writer:** drafted from this outline; founder rewrites and approves (to be confirmed, `questions.md` Q1). **Target:** about 500 words, one worked example, one evidence card, one link into the real explorer. Evidence for every claim below is in `research.md`.

**Guardrail (atlas §8):** the personal example must not funnel readers toward one societal answer. Don't present "possibility" as a solved mathematical idea; it is a working hypothesis about how people choose.

## Opening (1 paragraph)

When something is wrong, the first fix we think of is usually the obvious one, and often the only one. This page is about the other futures that open up when you ask what "better" would actually mean for you, and about what could make them reachable.

**You:** a sentence on why this idea is at the center of It for you. A moment where you, or someone you know, saw a future you hadn't considered. Keep it short; biography doesn't stand in for evidence.

## What is known (2 short paragraphs, sourced)

- People facing a decision tend to think of few options, and miss many of the ones they'd later say matter. Research on decision-making (Keeney's value-focused thinking; Siebert and Keeney's studies of how people generate alternatives; Nutt's studies of decisions inside organizations) is the evidence here. Label: **replicated finding** for "people list fewer than half of their own relevant options, and prompting with their goals helps" (`research.md` §1); these studies are about decisions in general, not life futures.
- Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum's capability approach asks what people are actually able to do and be, not only what they have. It also warns that people adjust what they want to what seems available ("adaptive preferences"). Say what they published; no implied endorsement.

## Our present view (label: working hypothesis)

Different goals and different situations lead to more futures than people first see. A pain can be the start of several different good lives, not one. Some of the ways there are things a person can do this week; some are things It could build; some are changes people would have to make together.

## A worked example: unemployment (the heart of the page)

Start from the pain as the explorer shows it: **Unemployment. "I can't find work."** Then show three or four genuinely different futures, for example:

- Finding work that suits me (the obvious one; keep it, and make it concrete).
- Getting by on my savings and benefits while I look, without panic.
- Work I chose, even if it isn't a regular job (self-employment, a cooperative).
- **I can live well regardless of employment.** This one reveals the most: it separates two things a job gives (income, and time structure, contact and purpose; Jahoda) and asks how to get each another way.

For that last future, show the three levels of fix, each with its status label:
1. **Now:** something a person can do today (for example, check unemployment insurance eligibility).
2. **Something It could build:** one feature, described as a proposal.
3. **A change we'd make together:** universal basic income, named plainly. One evidence card: what the trials found (Finland, the OpenResearch US study, Kenya) and what they couldn't test (cost, financing, a whole country at once). Say who supports and who opposes it, and name the alternatives people propose (job guarantee, stronger unemployment insurance, expanded tax credits).

**You:** which futures to show, and whether UBI is the right example at launch (`questions.md` Q3–Q5).

## Why it matters (1 paragraph)

If the first option is the only one people see, they judge their life by it. Seeing several real futures, with honest labels, gives a person more to choose from and gives people with the same future a reason to find each other.

## What remains open

- Whether showing more futures actually helps people act, or just overwhelms them. It hasn't tested this.
- How to tell a missing future from one nobody wants.
- Whether "possibility" can be described precisely. That is a research question (link `/research`), not a claim this page makes.
