# /future research

Research notes for the founder, 2026-10-20; not page copy.

Method: sources are original reports, papers, official pages, statutes and books, read on the owner's site where possible. Where only an abstract or publisher page was reachable, the note says so. Unverified or left out: the original wording and date of "Amara's law" (Roy Amara did not originate it); full texts of Ramírez & Wilkinson (2016), Collingridge (1980), Morozov (2013) and Dedehayir & Steinert (2016), so claims about them stay at abstract level; the full Grace et al. survey dates; and any figure for Metaculus questions, which this note does not use.

## 1. How good conditional scenarios are built

**Shell and Pierre Wack.** Wack, who led Shell's business-environment planning, wrote two HBR pieces in 1985 ("Scenarios: Uncharted Waters Ahead", September; "Scenarios: Shooting the Rapids", November). His starting point: forecast-based planning worked in the stable 1950s and 1960s, then failed by large margins after the early 1970s. Scenarios were meant to change how decision makers see, not to predict.

**Peter Schwartz, *The Art of the Long View* (1991).** A practitioner's handbook from the Shell tradition (summarized from the book, not re-checked for this note): find the decision, list driving forces, separate predetermined elements from critical uncertainties, build a small set of plots, then pick leading indicators to watch.

**Oxford Scenario Planning Approach.** Ramírez & Wilkinson (OUP, 2016) treat scenarios as tools for "reframing" an organization's view of its context. In a separate paper, Ramírez & Selin (2014) argue that the field's either/or split between plausibility and probability is unhelpful and that scenario work should be explicit about which one it is claiming. Only the abstract was read.

**IPCC.** Two practices matter here.
- The Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (O'Neill et al., 2017) are a set of five qualitative narratives of alternative societal futures (demographics, economy, policies and institutions, technology, environment), used side by side rather than ranked. I could not confirm a direct statement that no likelihoods were assigned, so do not quote one.
- The AR5 uncertainty guidance note (Mastrandrea et al., 2010) separates **confidence** (qualitative: evidence and agreement, five levels from "very low" to "very high", "not interpreted probabilistically") from **likelihood** (quantified, e.g. "likely" = 66–100%, "about as likely as not" = 33–66%). It says "about as likely as not" must not be used to express lack of knowledge; that findings conditional on other findings should have their certainty evaluated separately; and that authors give a "traceable account" of evidence, assumptions and agreement. It also warns against groups converging on a view and becoming overconfident.

**Futures cone.** Joseph Voros (2003, and his 2017 history of the cone) builds on Henchey (1978) and Hancock & Bezold (1994). Possible futures "might" happen (need knowledge we lack); plausible ones "could" happen given current understanding; probable ones are "likely", usually from trends; preferable ones "should" happen and are value judgments. Voros notes the classes are nested, judged from the present, and change over time, and that he mostly dropped "predicted" because it narrows openness.

**Dator's four generic futures.** Jim Dator (2009) describes the Manoa School's visioning process: people experience four generic alternatives (continuation, collapse, discipline, transformation) before stating a preferred future. The point is that a preferred future is chosen against named alternatives, including bad ones.

**Backcasting.** John Robinson coined "energy backcasting" (1982) and set out the method in "Futures under glass" (1990). The question is not what is likely but how a desirable future could be reached, working backward to the steps and conditions that would have to hold.

**UK Government Office for Science Futures Toolkit** (2017; edition 2, 2024). Its scenarios tool builds four scenarios from two independent critical uncertainties found through driver mapping, at least five and usually 10–20 years out. It states that "Scenarios are not predictions", that no single scenario will come true but parts of several may, that scenarios are possible futures and not a plan, and that names must not flatter the preferred one. Its Three Horizons tool links today's system (H1), an emerging pattern (H3) and the transition between them (H2).

**Practical rules extracted**
1. Claim plausibility, not probability, for scenarios. If a probability is given, it needs a stated method and evidence (IPCC; Ramírez & Selin).
2. Present a set, not one story. Each scenario is read against the others (Shell, SSPs, Futures Toolkit, Dator).
3. State drivers and assumptions explicitly, and separate what is near-certain from what is uncertain (Schwartz; IPCC traceable account).
4. Evaluate conditional chains link by link. "If A then B" needs confidence in A stated separately (IPCC).
5. Name signposts: observable things that would show a scenario is or is not unfolding (Schwartz; Futures Toolkit "early warning signs").
6. Label the type of future: possible, plausible, probable or preferable (Voros). A preferable future is a value statement and should be marked as one.
7. Use neutral names (Futures Toolkit).
8. For a preferred future, backcast: list what would have to be true, in order (Robinson).

## 2. Forecasts and evidence relevant to It

**Expert AI survey: Grace et al., "Thousands of AI Authors on the Future of AI" (arXiv, January 2024; revised 2025).** Who: 2,778 researchers who had published at top AI venues. Method: survey of individual judgments. Said: a 50% chance that unaided machines outperform humans at every task by 2047, 13 years earlier than the 2022 survey; full automation of all occupations at 10% by 2037 and 50% by 2116. Between 38% and 51% gave at least a 10% chance of outcomes as bad as human extinction. Caveat for /future: two closely related questions differ by about 70 years in the median, which suggests framing drives answers. This is opinion data, not a track record.

**Scenario-as-forecast: AI 2027 (Kokotajlo, Alexander, Larsen, Lifland, Dean; April 3, 2025).** Method: a narrative written period by period, informed by trend extrapolation (including METR task-length data), about 25 tabletop exercises and feedback from over 100 people. Two endings ("slowdown" and "race"). Assumptions: continued rapid growth in AI capability; a superhuman coder reached in March 2027. The authors call 2027 their modal year, say their medians were later (Lifland about 2030), and say the pace could be several times faster or slower. Per the site, a mid-2025 update moved medians later. Critics: titotal (June 19, 2025) argued the timeline models were poorly specified; the authors accepted some errors, paid a bounty, and said correcting the main one would move the median by about nine months. Lesson: even careful authors who state uncertainty get remembered for the vivid year.

**Trend extrapolation: Epoch AI, Sevilla & Roldán (May 28, 2024).** Training compute for notable AI models grew about 4.1x per year from 2010 to May 2024 (90% CI 3.7–4.6x). The authors offer it as a baseline "absent new bottlenecks or large breakthroughs", note a possible slowdown around 2018, and give wide intervals for recent periods. A good model of how to state a trend: rate, period, interval, and what would break it.

**Forecasting accuracy: Tetlock.** *Expert Political Judgment* (Princeton, 2005) tracked expert forecasts from tournaments run 1984–2003. In Gardner & Tetlock's own summary (Cato Unbound, July 2011), the average expert was "only slightly more accurate than random guessing" and slightly worse than simple extrapolation. The Good Judgment Project (Mellers et al., *Psychological Science*, 2014) found that training, teaming and tracking top performers improved accuracy, but on questions mostly resolving within about a year. A 2025 reanalysis in the same journal disputes the size of the training and teaming effects. No source here shows skill at forecasting social or technological change decades out.

**Institutions: Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990).** From field cases, eight design principles of long-lasting common-pool resource institutions: clear boundaries; rules fitted to local conditions; users take part in changing rules; monitoring; graduated sanctions; cheap conflict resolution; outside authorities recognize the right to organize; nested layers for larger systems. Cox, Arnold & Villamayor-Tomás (*Ecology and Society*, 2010) reviewed 91 studies and found the principles well supported. Relevance: community ownership is a design problem with known requirements, not a feature switch.

**Ownership: worker co-ops and ESOPs.** Pérotin's review for Co-operatives UK (2016) concludes that worker co-operatives survive at least as long as conventional firms and are more productive than often assumed; she has called the survival evidence thin. NCEO (page updated January 2026, 2023 data) counts 6,609 US ESOPs at 6,411 companies, with 15.1 million participants and about $2.06 trillion in plan assets. ESOPs are mostly a succession tool, so they show that broad ownership can be durable at scale, not that user-owned platforms work.

**Platform cooperativism: Scholz (Rosa Luxemburg Stiftung, 2016).** A proposal and critique, not evidence of outcomes: platforms owned by workers or users as an alternative to the "sharing economy." Treat it as an argument.

**Data portability: law and working systems.**
- GDPR Article 20 (2016, applies from 2018): people may receive data they provided in a structured, machine-readable format and send it to another controller, directly where technically feasible, when processing is based on consent or contract and automated.
- EU Digital Markets Act (Regulation 2022/1925), Article 6(9): designated gatekeepers must provide effective, continuous, real-time portability of user-provided and activity-generated data, free of charge. Details here are from secondary legal summaries; check the text before quoting.
- Data Transfer Project (Google, Microsoft, Facebook, Twitter; announced July 20, 2018): open-source adapters for direct service-to-service transfer.
- AT Protocol (Bluesky): an official guide documents moving an account, its repository and identity, to another host, including a recovery path when the old host does not cooperate. The guide says these mechanisms are not formally part of the protocol and may change.
Relevance: portability is implemented in law and in at least one deployed protocol, but switching providers with full history and social context is still partial.

**Governance failure: The DAO (2016).** The SEC's Section 21(a) report (July 25, 2017) records that The DAO raised about 12 million ETH (about $150 million) and that an attacker used a code flaw to take about one-third of it. Ethereum then hard-forked to recover the funds; a minority kept the old chain. Feichtinger et al. (2023) studied on-chain governance in 21 DAOs and found voting power concentrated in few holders, hidden costs, and much "pointless" governance activity. Lesson: code-enforced governance does not by itself produce broad, safe participation.

## 3. Failure modes of techno-optimist roadmaps

**Long-range forecasting has little demonstrated skill** (Tetlock, above). Implication: no dated milestones beyond what It controls.

**"Amara's law"** (overestimate short-run effects, underestimate long-run). Quote Investigator (January 2019) traces the idea to 1965, when J. C. R. Licklider called it a "modern maxim," and finds Amara linked to it only from 1983. Implication: if used, attribute it as an anonymous maxim, and note it is a heuristic, not evidence.

**Gartner hype cycle.** Gartner's own branded model. Dedehayir & Steinert (*Technological Forecasting and Social Change*, 2016) checked Gartner's data and reported inconsistencies with the model; later reviews still find little empirical support. Implication: do not use it as a law.

**Solutionism.** Morozov (*To Save Everything, Click Here*, 2013): treating complex social problems as having simple, often technical fixes, presuming the problem rather than studying it. Implication: each horizon should say what It would not solve and who else has to act.

**Cybersyn.** Medina (*Cybernetic Revolutionaries*, MIT Press, 2011): Allende's Chile tried to build a near real-time economic management system; it was never fully implemented, and the 1973 coup ended it. Implication: coordination technology depends on politics and institutions outside the product.

**Project Xanadu.** Wolf (*Wired*, June 1995) called Ted Nelson's universal hypertext project "the longest-running vaporware project in the history of computing," while the Web shipped a simpler version. Implication: an ambitious vision with no shipped intermediate steps loses to smaller working ones.

**Facebook, "connecting the world."** In June 2017 Facebook changed its mission to "give people the power to build community and bring the world closer together." Its November 2018 post on the BSR human rights assessment of Myanmar said that before 2018 "we weren't doing enough" to keep the platform from being used to foment division and incite offline violence. Implication: a coordination tool's harms scale with its success; the essay should name harms for each horizon.

**Collingridge dilemma** (*The Social Control of Technology*, 1980): early on, impacts are hard to know; once a technology is entrenched, it is hard to change. Implication: say which decisions It is fixing now (privacy invariants, governance) because they get harder to change later.

**Sociotechnical imaginaries.** Jasanoff & Kim (*Dreamscapes of Modernity*, 2015): "collectively held, institutionally stabilized, and publicly performed visions of desirable futures." Visions shape what gets built and who benefits. Implication: /future is itself one of these; it should say whose future it describes and invite others to contest it.

**Planning fallacy and reference classes.** Kahneman & Tversky (1979) described underestimating time and cost by planning from the inside view. Flyvbjerg (*Project Management Journal*, 2006) proposed reference class forecasting: base estimates on outcomes of comparable projects. Implication: compare It's plans to what small, solo-founded civic or cooperative tech projects have actually done, not to the plan.

## Implications for /future

- Call every horizon a scenario and say in the first lines that it is neither a prediction nor a commitment. Give no dates except for work already shipped or scheduled.
- Write the three horizons as a set, each with its own drivers, assumptions and failure case. Include at least one outcome where It stays small or stops.
- For each horizon, list "what would have to be true" as a backcast chain, and label each link implemented, prototype, unresolved research or speculative. Rate the chain link by link, never as a whole.
- Use plausibility language ("could", "would require"). Use probability words only with a stated method and source, and never "about as likely as not" to mean "we don't know."
- Give each horizon two or three signposts a reader could check, such as working account export or a governance rule changed by members.
- Cite outside forecasts only with who, when, method and key assumption, and say how far they reach. Prefer trend data with stated intervals (Epoch) over opinion surveys, and note Tetlock's finding on long horizons.
- For each horizon, name likely harms and who else must act (institutions, law, other projects).
- Separate preferable from plausible. Mark the founder's hopes as hopes.
- Use neutral names for horizons. Avoid hype words and cite no "laws" (Amara, hype cycle) as evidence.
- Treat community ownership and portability as design problems with known requirements (Ostrom, GDPR Art. 20, AT Protocol), not as promises.
