# MVP Societal Pains — Source Registry

## Purpose

This document is the research map for discovering, validating, and prioritizing **societal pains** for the We Are It / It MVP.

It is **not** the canonical list of pains. Instead, it records where candidate pains come from, what kind of evidence each source provides, which populations it represents, and what we have extracted from it.

The goal is broad coverage without confusing:

- what people **say bothers them**;
- what researchers **measure as harmful or burdensome**;
- what people **want changed**;
- what experts or institutions **interpret as a social problem**; and
- our own hypotheses about what ought to count as a pain.

A pain can be important even when people do not spontaneously name it, but those are different kinds of evidence and should remain distinguishable.

---

## Evidence Types

### A. Directly elicited pains

Sources that ask people what worries, frustrates, burdens, threatens, or matters to them.

Examples:

- open-ended “most important problem” questions;
- issue-priority polling;
- quality-of-life surveys;
- community needs assessments;
- qualitative interviews;
- focus groups;
- complaint or grievance datasets.

**Best use:** discovering language and concerns that people themselves recognize.

### B. Experienced conditions

Sources measuring conditions plausibly experienced as pains without necessarily asking respondents to label them as such.

Examples:

- loneliness and social isolation;
- financial insecurity;
- housing cost burden;
- commute burden;
- healthcare access;
- discrimination;
- exposure to crime or pollution;
- work stress;
- caregiving burden.

**Best use:** finding widespread problems that issue-priority polling may miss.

### C. Psychological and social needs

Research on recurring human needs, well-being, motivation, belonging, agency, trust, meaning, status, security, autonomy, competence, and related constructs.

**Best use:** identifying deeper pains beneath surface complaints and checking whether the corpus covers major dimensions of human flourishing.

### D. Behavioral traces and revealed problems

Observed behavior that can reveal unmet needs or recurring friction.

Examples:

- search trends;
- 311/service requests;
- consumer complaints;
- help-line topics;
- mutual-aid requests;
- labor grievances;
- public comments;
- online discussion corpora.

**Best use:** discovering concrete, specific problems that broad surveys smooth over.

### E. Social indicators and administrative data

Population-level measures of social conditions.

Examples:

- poverty and inequality;
- housing;
- health;
- employment;
- education;
- environment;
- crime and safety;
- mobility;
- family structure;
- civic participation.

**Best use:** prevalence, severity, distribution, and trend validation.

### F. Expert or institutional problem frameworks

Research syntheses, policy reports, public-health frameworks, sociological analyses, and other expert attempts to characterize social problems.

**Best use:** discovering omissions, causal structure, affected populations, and useful distinctions.

**Caution:** expert-defined problems are not equivalent to experienced or self-reported pains.

## Source Registry

For each source we investigate, record:

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Source | Organization, study, dataset, paper, or corpus |
| Type | A–F from the evidence taxonomy above |
| Population | Who is represented |
| Geography | Geographic coverage |
| Date / cadence | Field date, publication date, and whether repeated |
| Method | Survey, interview, administrative data, behavioral trace, etc. |
| Question / construct | Exact question wording or measured construct when relevant |
| Open-ended? | Whether respondents can supply concerns rather than choose from a predefined list |
| Candidate pains | Pains extracted or supported |
| Strength | Discovery / prevalence / severity / trend / causal context / language |
| Limitations | Sampling, framing, forced-choice effects, interpretation, age, geography, etc. |
| Status | To review / reviewed / extracted / incorporated |
| Link / citation | Stable source location |
| Notes | Anything else needed for provenance |

## Discovery Strategy

The first extraction pass should not treat all sources equally. Use them in layers:

**Layer 1 — Generate vocabulary.** Start with Gallup's open-ended Most Important Problem series, complaint/grievance corpora, qualitative studies, and open-ended community needs work. These are our best defenses against building a taxonomy entirely from researcher-supplied answer choices.

**Layer 2 — Expand coverage.** Use Healthy People 2030, NHIS, SHED, HTOPS, ATUS, AHS, GSS, YRBS, education surveys, caregiving research, crime/victimization data, and environmental measures to find burdens people may not spontaneously call “the country's biggest problem.”

**Layer 3 — Validate salience and prevalence.** Use Pew issue batteries, KFF, federal statistical series and other representative surveys to determine which candidate pains are widely recognized or experienced and by whom.

**Layer 4 — Check cultural and population blind spots.** Use WVS, OECD sources, regional barometers, subgroup-specific surveys, qualitative work, and local needs assessments.

**Layer 5 — Preserve disagreement.** If different populations experience, name, or interpret a problem differently, keep that structure. Do not average the disagreement away merely to obtain a cleaner checkbox.

### First-pass extraction target

Rather than trying to pick the final ~50–60 societal pains immediately, extract a deliberately redundant **research corpus of roughly 150–250 candidate pain concepts**. Preserve aliases and source wording. We can then cluster, split, test coverage, and write user-facing MVP language after we know what the evidence space actually contains.


## Extraction Rules

1. **Preserve respondent language whenever possible.** Store the original wording before normalizing it into an MVP label.
2. **Keep source and inference separate.** “42% report X” and “therefore the pain is Y” are different records.
3. **Do not equate salience with severity.** A problem can be severe but rarely named spontaneously, or highly salient but affect relatively few people.
4. **Do not equate prevalence with universality.** Preserve population and geography.
5. **Record question framing.** Forced-choice lists can manufacture apparent priorities by determining what respondents are allowed to select.
6. **Prefer repeated and triangulated findings.** One poll can discover a candidate; multiple methods strengthen confidence that it belongs in the corpus.
7. **Retain minority pains.** The MVP should not erase serious problems merely because they affect smaller populations.
8. **Distinguish pain from proposed cause and solution.** “Rent is too high,” “zoning causes high rent,” and “build more housing” are different objects.
9. **Distinguish personal from societal framing.** “I can't afford rent” and “housing is unaffordable” may connect, but should not automatically collapse into one item.
10. **Track omissions explicitly.** “None of these” and open-ended responses are evidence about the limits of our taxonomy.

## Candidate Evaluation Dimensions

When we later decide which pains belong in the MVP, useful dimensions include:

- **prevalence** — how many people experience or identify it;
- **severity** — how consequential it is for those affected;
- **salience** — whether people recognize and name it;
- **breadth** — how many populations or contexts it spans;
- **specificity** — whether the label means roughly the same thing to respondents;
- **actionability** — whether It can eventually route toward meaningful action;
- **generativity** — whether selecting it helps reveal useful downstream distinctions;
- **coverage contribution** — whether it captures something not already represented;
- **evidence diversity** — whether different methods converge on it.

These are descriptive dimensions, not yet a scoring formula.

## Provenance Workflow

For every candidate societal pain:

`raw source evidence → extracted concern/condition → normalized pain concept → relationships/aliases → MVP wording`

We should retain enough provenance to travel backward from an MVP checkbox to the evidence that motivated it.

A future pain record should therefore be able to answer:

- Where did this come from?
- Who expressed or experienced it?
- How was it measured?
- When and where?
- What was the original wording?
- What transformations did we make?
- What related pains did we merge or keep separate?
- Why is it in or out of the MVP?
