# Generator finalists: 16 pilot pains (after repair)

Generated 2026-10-19 by the possibilities extension v0.5. Each pain was written by a fresh writer agent. A repair pass then made every number and name in the copy trace to an opened source, and three isolated judges ranked the cards by consensus. Honesty exclusions fell from 39 to 5. **Nothing here has been reviewed by the founder yet.** Status labels are in brackets.

### Sleep: “I don't sleep well.”

Run `docs/research/possibilities/runs/poor-sleep/20261019-142010` · 14 cards · 18/21 examples verified

**1. Falling asleep without pills or dread**: *I go to bed expecting to sleep, and most nights I do.*
- **Ask your doctor about short, drug-free insomnia therapy** [Existing resource] In a large trial in England’s health service, four short sessions with a practice nurse helped people with insomnia more than the usual advice leaflet, without pills. This therapy isn’t right for everyone, so the nurse checks first. *First step:* Write down when you went to bed and got up for the next week, so you have something to bring.
- **Try an online insomnia course your doctor recommends** [Existing resource] England’s health guidance body recommended an online course called Sleepio over sleeping pills in 2022, after reviewing 12 trials. You do the course on your phone or computer, in your own time. *First step:* Ask your doctor at your next visit whether there’s an online insomnia course they’d recommend.
- **Keep a simple morning sleep diary** [Something It could build] Sleep experts agreed on a standard nine-question morning diary in 2012, and it’s what therapists ask for first. It could ask you those questions each morning and turn the answers into a summary you can show a doctor. *First step:* Tell us you’d use this, and tomorrow morning jot down when you went to bed and when you woke up.

**2. Evenings free from work messages**: *When I finish work, I’m actually finished, and my phone stays quiet until morning.*
- **Agree on quiet hours with your team** [Real now] Portugal passed a law in 2021, in effect since 2022: outside working hours, employers aren’t supposed to contact staff except in a real emergency. Your team doesn’t need a law to agree on the same thing. *First step:* Suggest a simple rule, like no work messages between 8pm and 8am, and see who’s in.
- **Ask lawmakers for a law that protects time off** [A change we’d make together] Under Portugal’s law, contacting staff during their rest time counts as a serious violation. There’s no data yet on how well the law is enforced or whether people sleep better, but it puts the rule in writing instead of leaving it to how considerate your boss happens to be. *First step:* Send your representative a short note about Portugal’s law and why you’d want one here.

**3. A bedroom that stays dark and quiet all night**: *Streetlights, early sun and traffic noise stop waking me up.*
- **Put up blackout curtains or wear an eye mask** [Real now] A Northwestern study found that one night with a room light on raised people’s heart rate and made their bodies handle sugar worse the next morning. The researchers’ rule of thumb: if you can see things clearly in your bedroom, it’s too bright. *First step:* Stand in your bedroom tonight with the lights off and notice where light gets in.
- **Run a steady sound like pink or white noise** [Real now] A smooth background sound makes sudden noises like a door or a car less jarring. A 2022 review of 34 studies found the research thin, but pink noise did better than white noise and nobody found it harmful. *First step:* Play a free pink-noise track at low volume tonight and notice whether you wake up less.
- **Try sleep earbuds that cover up snoring and traffic** [Existing resource] Earbuds made for sleep, such as Ozlo’s, seal out some sound and play quiet masking noise over the rest. They cost about $279, so foam earplugs are worth trying first. *First step:* Buy a pack of foam earplugs and try them for three nights.

**4. Sleeping in separate rooms so we both rest**: *My partner and I both sleep through the night, even if that means separate rooms some nights.*
- **Try sleeping apart for two weeks, then talk about it** [Real now] More than a third of American adults sleep in a different room from their partner at least some of the time. Sleeping apart is common, and it doesn’t have to mean anything is wrong between you. *First step:* Pick a start date together and decide who gets which room.
- **Have the person who snores checked for sleep apnea** [Existing resource] Loud snoring is sometimes a treatable condition rather than just a habit, and treating it can help both of you. *First step:* Book a doctor’s appointment and mention the snoring and any daytime sleepiness.

**5. Knowing how much noise reaches my bedroom**: *I know whether my street is actually loud at night, and which fixes are worth trying.*
- **Check which kinds of noise reach your address** [Something It could build] The US Department of Transportation publishes a map of airport and highway noise, down to the county. It could look up your address on maps like that, tell you which kinds of noise are likely to reach it, and suggest fixes that match, from earplugs to a neighbors’ complaint. *First step:* Write down the three sounds that wake you most often, and roughly when.
- **Record the noise in your bedroom for one night** [Real now] Knowing whether it’s trucks at 2am or a neighbor’s TV at midnight changes what will actually help. Many phones have free sound-level apps that log noise overnight. *First step:* Install a free sound-level app and leave it recording on your nightstand tonight.

### Broke: “I'm always short on money.”

**1. Hospital bills cut or forgiven when I can’t pay**: *When a hospital bill comes, I find out whether I qualify to have it reduced or erased before I pay anything.*
- **Ask the hospital for its financial assistance application** [Existing resource] Nonprofit hospitals have to offer charity care that reduces or erases bills for patients under an income limit, and each hospital sets its own limit. Asking for the application is how you find out where you stand. *First step:* Search your hospital’s name with the words financial assistance policy, and download the application.
- **Let a free nonprofit file the application for you** [Existing resource] Dollar For, a nonprofit, prepares charity care applications, sends them to the hospital and follows up, at no cost to patients. The group says it has helped clear more than $38 million in medical bills since 2019. *First step:* Answer the eligibility questions on Dollar For’s website with your household size and income.
- **Ask for an itemized bill before you pay** [Real now] An itemized bill lists every charge, so you can spot duplicates or services you never received. Ask for it before you agree to a payment plan. *First step:* Call the billing number on your statement and ask for an itemized bill with the billing codes.

**2. Getting every tax credit my family is owed**: *When I file my taxes, I get back every credit we qualify for without paying someone to find them.*
- **Check whether you qualify for the earned income tax credit** [Existing resource] The IRS estimates that about one in five people who qualify for this credit don’t claim it. For tax year 2024, families with children could get up to $7,830, and the average credit the year before was $2,743. *First step:* Find last year’s tax return and look for the line labeled earned income credit to see whether you claimed it.
- **Have your taxes done free at a volunteer tax site** [Existing resource] The IRS supports free tax preparation sites staffed by trained volunteers, mainly for people earning about $70,244 or less. The sites mostly serve people who qualify for the earned income credit, so the volunteers know to look for it. *First step:* Search for the IRS VITA site locator and write down the closest site and its hours.
- **File past returns to claim credits you missed** [Existing resource] In general, you have three years from a return’s due date to claim a refund, including refundable credits you didn’t know about. Many volunteer sites can prepare past-year returns too. *First step:* Make a list of which of the last three years you didn’t file, or filed without claiming any credits.

**3. Borrowing from people I trust while building my credit**: *When I need a lump sum, I borrow from a circle of people I know, and my credit score goes up while I repay.*
- **Join a lending circle that reports to the credit bureaus** [Real now] In San Francisco, Mission Asset Fund runs lending circles where six to ten people each pay in monthly and take turns receiving the pot, with payments reported to the credit bureaus. In one study, members who started with no credit score or a poor one were almost twice as likely as a comparison group to raise their score by 20 points or more. *First step:* Look up Mission Asset Fund’s lending circles and check whether they serve your state.
- **Start a lending circle with friends or coworkers** [Something It could build] Many families already do this informally, under names like tanda, susu or hui. It could let a group set the amount and the order, keep a shared record, and remind everyone when a payment is due. *First step:* Tell us you’d use this, and think of three people you’d trust to pay in every month.

**4. Knowing exactly what leaves my account each month**: *I know every bill and subscription that comes out of my account, and none of them catch me off guard.*
- **List every charge that repeats on your statements** [Real now] In a 2022 survey of 1,000 Americans, people guessed they spent about $86 a month on subscriptions, but when they listed them out the average was $219. Forty-two percent were still paying for something they had stopped using. *First step:* Open your last two bank or card statements and highlight every charge that shows up both months.
- **Give each dollar a job before the month starts** [Real now] A common starting point is the 50/30/20 split: about half of take-home pay for needs, 30% for wants, and 20% for savings or debt. If needs alone take more than half, you’re far from alone, and the bigger fix is more likely raising income or lowering one large bill than cutting small treats. *First step:* Write your take-home pay for one month at the top of a page, and list rent, utilities, food and transportation under it.
- **Cancel unused subscriptions in writing and save the proof** [Real now] A federal rule that would have made canceling as easy as signing up was struck down in court in July 2025, so some companies still make it hard. A dated email or screenshot of your cancellation helps a lot if you’re charged again. *First step:* Pick the subscription you use least, cancel it today, and save a screenshot of the confirmation.

**5. Knowing every benefit my household qualifies for**: *I know which programs we qualify for, what each one is worth, and how to apply.*
- **Check which benefits your household qualifies for** [Something It could build] It could ask a few questions about your household and estimate what you’re eligible for, the way MyFriendBen does in Colorado, where the nonprofit says its tool has helped more than 55,000 households apply for $33 million in benefits. Your answers would stay on your device. *First step:* Tell us you’d use this, and write down your household size and monthly income before taxes.
- **Apply for food benefits online** [Existing resource] In California, Code for America's GetCalFresh cut the online application for food benefits from about 45 minutes to under 10. A faster form doesn't mean a faster decision, so it's worth applying as soon as you can. *First step:* Search your state’s name with SNAP application and see whether you can start online today.
- **Keep track of renewal dates so benefits don’t lapse** [Something It could build] Food, health and phone benefits each renew on their own schedule, and one missed letter can end coverage. It could keep those dates in one place and remind you a few weeks ahead. *First step:* Tell us you’d use this, and find the most recent renewal letter for any benefit you get.

### Procrastination: “I put things off.”

Run `docs/research/possibilities/runs/procrastination/20261019-142011` · 14 cards · 19/19 examples verified

**1. Getting boring tasks done with someone else there**: *I sit down with a friend, or on a video call with a stranger, and we both get through the tasks we’ve been avoiding.*
- **Do your task while a friend does theirs** [Real now] Having another person nearby, even one doing something completely different, can make it easier to stay with a dull or uncomfortable task. An ADHD coach named this body doubling, and people without ADHD use it too. *First step:* Text a friend and ask if they’d like to spend an hour this week working on your own lists side by side.
- **Book a video work session with a stranger** [Existing resource] Focusmate pairs you on video with another person for 25, 50 or 75 minutes: you each say what you’ll work on, work quietly, and check in at the end. Three sessions a week are free. *First step:* Book one 25-minute session for the task you’ve been putting off longest.
- **Put money on a deadline with a friend as referee** [Existing resource] On stickK, a site started in 2008 by two Yale economists, you set a goal, pick a friend to confirm whether you did it, and put money on it that goes elsewhere if you miss. The co-founder reported that 78% of people who used both money and a referee reached their goal, compared with 35% of those who put no money down. *First step:* Choose one task, a date, and a friend you’d trust to say honestly whether you did it.

**2. Letting go of tasks I don’t actually need to do**: *I’ve stopped carrying around tasks I was never going to do, and the ones left on my list are ones I mean.*
- **Cross off a task you’ve already decided against** [Real now] A task that has sat on the list for months is sometimes one you’ve quietly decided not to do. Crossing it off on purpose frees up the attention it keeps taking every time you see it. *First step:* Read your to-do list and cross off one item you’ve kept for months and don’t want to do.
- **Flip a coin on a decision you’ve put off for months** [Real now] In a study published in 2021, the economist Steven Levitt had people who were stuck on a big decision, like quitting a job or a class, let a coin toss guide them. Those the coin told to make the change were happier six months later than those it told to stay put. *First step:* Write the decision as a yes-or-no question, flip a coin, and notice whether you feel relieved or disappointed.
- **Pay or trade someone to do a task you dread** [Real now] If a task matters but you’ve dreaded it for a year, someone else can often do it for a fee or a trade, like a tax preparer, a handyman, or a friend who enjoys paperwork. Paying for it is a reasonable trade when the dread has been costing you for months. *First step:* Write down one task you’d happily pay to never think about again, and look up what it would cost.

**3. Understanding the feeling that makes me put things off**: *When I notice I’m avoiding something, I can name what it makes me feel and still take the first step.*
- **Name the feeling the task brings up** [Real now] The psychologists Sirois and Pychyl argued in 2013 that putting things off is mostly about getting away from a bad feeling right now, like boredom, worry or self-doubt, rather than a lack of willpower. Knowing which feeling you’re dodging often tells you what would make the task easier, such as company for a boring job or a second opinion for a scary one. *First step:* Write the task down, and next to it write the one word for how it makes you feel.
- **Forgive yourself for the last time you put it off** [Real now] In a 2010 study of 134 first-year college students, the ones who forgave themselves for putting off studying for their first exams put off studying less before the next round. Being hard on yourself tends to make the task feel worse, and the students who went easier on themselves did better. *First step:* Say or write one sentence letting yourself off the hook for the last delay, then look at what’s next.

**4. A clear reminder before deadlines that cost me to miss**: *Before a court date, renewal or benefits deadline, I get a reminder that says when, where and what happens if I miss it.*
- **Ask your city or county for plain text reminders** [A change we’d make together] In New York City in 2018, text reminders that explained the consequences and asked people to plan how they’d get there cut missed court dates by 26%, and with a redesigned summons form the drop was 36%. Many missed dates are simply forgotten, and a better reminder is cheap to send. *First step:* Check whether your county court or motor vehicle office offers text reminders, and sign up if it does.
- **Get reminders for every deadline with a penalty** [Something It could build] It could keep track of the dates that cost money or worse to miss, like court dates, license renewals and benefit recertifications, and send the kind of plan-ahead reminder that New York City tested. *First step:* Tell us you’d use this, and list the three deadlines this year that would cost you most to miss.

**5. Understanding which kind of putting off I’m doing**: *I can tell whether I’m avoiding a feeling, stuck on a step, holding a task I don’t want, or dealing with something like ADHD, and I know what to try for each.*
- **Sort the tasks you’ve put off by why they’re stuck** [Something It could build] It could walk you through a few questions about each task you’ve been putting off: does it bring up a feeling, is it waiting on someone, do you still want it done, and does this happen with almost everything. Each answer points to a different kind of fix, drawing on research like Sirois and Pychyl’s, without pretending to diagnose you. *First step:* Tell us you’d use this, and write down the three tasks you’ve put off longest.
- **Keep a private log of what you put off** [Something It could build] It could keep a simple log, on your device, of what you put off and what finally got it moving, so after a few weeks you can see whether it’s mostly phone calls, paperwork, or nearly everything. That pattern is useful for you and for a doctor if you decide to see one. *First step:* For the next week, jot down each thing you put off and what you did instead.
- **Bring a clear summary to your doctor** [Something It could build] If the pattern shows up at work, with bills and at home over many years, It could help you put together a short list of real examples to take to a doctor. The National Institute of Mental Health describes ADHD as starting in childhood, so examples from school help too. *First step:* Tell us you’d use this, and write down one example from school and one from this month.

### Representation: “I'm not heard by the people in charge.”

**1. Deciding with neighbors how some city money gets spent**: *My neighbors and I vote on what gets built in our part of town, and then we see it built.*
- **Vote in participatory budgeting where your city offers it** [Real now] In participatory budgeting, residents propose and vote on projects paid for with real public money. In New York City’s 2016–17 round, 102,800 residents voted on how to spend over $40 million across 31 council districts. *First step:* Search your city council’s website for “participatory budgeting” to see if your district takes part.
- **Ask your council member to let residents decide part of their budget** [A change we’d make together] In 2009 Alderman Joe Moore of Chicago’s 49th Ward handed residents about $1 million of his ward’s capital money; more than 1,300 people voted, funding beach showers, heated bus shelters and a dog park. Skeptics point out that small organized groups can win more than their share, so turnout matters. *First step:* Email your council member’s office and ask whether they’d try participatory budgeting next year.
- **Get told when your city opens a vote on spending** [Something It could build] It could keep track of which cities and districts run participatory budgeting and let you know when proposals or voting open near you. Cities already publish their schedules, but they’re scattered across council websites. *First step:* Tell us you’d use this.

**2. Randomly chosen residents advising on hard questions**: *When my state faces a hard question, a group of ordinary residents drawn by lot studies it, and lawmakers have to answer what they recommend.*
- **Learn how a randomly chosen citizens’ assembly works** [Real now] In 2016 Ireland drew 99 citizens at random, matched to the census by age, gender, class and region, to study the abortion amendment in its constitution. Most of them, 64%, recommended a change, and in the 2018 referendum 66.4% of voters agreed. People still disagree strongly about the outcome; what drew attention elsewhere was the process. *First step:* Read a one-page summary of the Irish assembly and write down one question you’d want such a group in your state to take on.
- **Ask lawmakers for a permanent citizens’ panel** [A change we’d make together] Since 2019 the German-speaking region of Belgium has had a 24-person citizens’ council, drawn by lot, that picks topics for citizens’ panels. Their advice isn’t binding, but parliament must debate it twice within a year. *First step:* Write your state legislator a short note asking whether they’d support a trial citizens’ panel on one local issue.
- **Ask what lawmakers must do with the panel’s advice** [A change we’d make together] France’s citizens’ convention on climate made 149 recommendations in 2020. The president rejected 3 outright and the climate bill fully included 46, which left many participants disappointed. A panel works best when everyone knows ahead of time what the government owes it. *First step:* In your note, ask what the legislature would be required to do with the panel’s recommendations.

**3. Voting in the local elections where a few votes decide**: *I vote for mayor, city council and school board, not just for president, because those races are often decided by very few people.*
- **Put the next local election date on your calendar** [Real now] A Portland State University study of four cities found mayoral turnout as low as 7% of registered voters, in Charlotte’s primary. When so few people vote, each vote is a much bigger share of the result. *First step:* Look up your city’s next election date on your county elections website and add it to your calendar.
- **Ask a younger friend to vote with you** [Real now] The same study found registered voters 65 and older were about 10 times as likely to vote in those mayoral races as voters 18 to 34. Whoever shows up is who local officials hear from, so a couple of friends who start voting changes who that is. *First step:* Text one friend who usually skips local elections and ask if they want to go vote together.

**4. Getting a real answer from my representative’s office**: *When I contact my representative, a staff member reads what I wrote and I hear back.*
- **Write a short personal message instead of a form letter** [Real now] In surveys by the Congressional Management Foundation, 92% of congressional staff said an individual email from a constituent would have some or a lot of influence on a lawmaker who hadn’t decided. Say where you live, the one issue, and what it means for you or your family. *First step:* Write three sentences: who you are and where you live, the one issue, and how it affects you.
- **Call the office and ask for the staff member on your issue** [Real now] Every office has staff who each cover a set of issues, and asking for that person gets your message to someone who briefs the member. The House switchboard at (202) 224-3121 connects you to any member’s office, and the member’s website lists the office back home. *First step:* Call the office, give your ZIP code, and ask the name of the staff member who handles your issue.
- **Send one message to all your officials by text** [Existing resource] Resistbot is a free service: you text “resist” to 50409, answer a few questions, and your message goes to your senators, representative, governor, state legislators and mayor. Free messages go by email, and postal mail or fax costs extra. *First step:* Text “resist” to 50409 and send the three sentences you wrote.

**5. Neighbors who go to city hall together**: *When something in our neighborhood needs to change, a few of us show up together, and the council hears from all of us.*
- **Find neighbors who want the same thing changed** [Something It could build] It could let people on the same street or in the same neighborhood mark the issue they want fixed, and introduce them to each other once a few people share it. Nobody would see your name until you chose to meet. *First step:* Tell us you’d use this.
- **Register the same position together on one bill** [Real now] In Washington State, each person can sign in online to a committee hearing with their position, so ten neighbors signing in on the same bill show up as ten residents. Agree on the bill and the day, then each person signs in with a sentence in their own words. *First step:* Send two neighbors the bill and hearing date and ask if they’ll sign in too.
- **Go to a neighborhood budget meeting as a group** [Real now] In one year of participatory budgeting, Chicago’s 49th Ward held nine neighborhood meetings, and about 400 people came to shape the projects later put to a vote. Coming as a group with one prepared idea gives the meeting something concrete to work with. *First step:* Ask your council member’s office when the next neighborhood meeting on spending is.

### Coordination: “I can't find who's already working on this.”

**1. Meeting the neighbors who care about the same thing**: *I find out that four people on my side of town want the same crosswalk I do.*
- **Say privately what you care about and meet others once enough do** [Something It could build] In Vermont, each new Front Porch Forum stays in a waiting stage until 100 residents sign up, so nobody posts to an empty room. It could work the same way: you say privately what you care about, and only when enough people nearby have said the same would It offer to introduce you, with your permission. *First step:* Tell us you'd use this, and name the one local issue you would want to meet neighbors about.

A short note to a neighborhood list naming one issue, one place and one time is how many neighborhood groups began. *First step:* Draft a three-sentence invitation naming the issue, a place and a date, and send it to one neighborhood list.

**2. Knowing which local groups already work on my issue**: *When something in my town bothers me, I can name the groups already working on it.*
- **Search nonprofit tax filings for your town and issue** [Real now] ProPublica's free Nonprofit Explorer lets you search the full text of the tax forms nonprofits file each year, so a search for your town plus a word like "tenants" or "river" turns up groups whose names never mention it. Small informal groups that never filed won't show up, so treat the results as a starting list. *First step:* Search Nonprofit Explorer for your town and one word that describes your issue, and write down the first three groups.
- **Call or text 211 when the issue is a basic need** [Existing resource] If what you care about is food, housing, rides or health care, 211 is a free, confidential line answered by local specialists, and about 13 million requests reach it each year. The people there know which local groups are actually doing that work. *First step:* Dial 211 and ask which groups near you work on the need you care about.
- **Look at volunteer listings near you** [Real now] Groups that need help tend to post it. Idealist, a long-running nonprofit directory, has posted 450,000 volunteer openings over the years, and a search by your ZIP code shows who is active right now. *First step:* Search Idealist for volunteer openings near your ZIP code and note which groups keep showing up.

**3. Seeing who has already raised my issue at city hall**: *Before I speak up, I know who has spoken about this at council meetings and what they asked for.*
- **Search past city council meetings for your issue** [Real now] Council Data Project, started by a University of Washington professor and a student after they followed a Seattle City Council vote on an arena, turns council meetings in 17 cities into searchable transcripts. People who spoke during public comment are often the ones already organizing, and their names are in the record. *First step:* Search your city's meeting minutes or council transcripts for your issue and note who spoke about it.
- **Look up whether your state lawmakers have taken up the issue** [Real now] Open States, a free tool from Plural, shows your state lawmakers from your address and keeps a record of state bills. A bill on your issue usually comes with groups that testified for or against it. *First step:* Enter your address in the Open States lookup and write down your two state lawmakers.

**4. Local groups on my issue working from one plan**: *The groups in my town that work on the same problem agree on goals and stop duplicating each other.*
- **Ask local funders whether a shared plan exists for your issue** [Real now] In Cincinnati, more than 300 leaders from foundations, city government, school districts, colleges and nonprofits agreed to track the same 53 student measures, and four years after the effort began, 34 of them were trending up. Community foundations often know whether something like this already exists for your issue. *First step:* Look up your area's community foundation and email to ask whether a coalition already works on your issue.
- **Map the groups on your issue with a few neighbors** [Real now] Kretzmann and McKnight's asset-mapping method starts by listing every local association by type and asking which are most likely to work together. A one-page map is often the first thing that lets separate groups see each other. *First step:* Start a shared document listing every group you know of on your issue, and invite two people to add to it.

**5. Hearing what my neighbors are already organizing**: *I hear about the cleanup, the petition or the meeting before it happens, not after.*
- **Sign up for your neighborhood email list or forum** [Real now] In Vermont, Front Porch Forum started in Burlington in 2006 as a moderated neighborhood bulletin board, and by 2013 every town could have one, with 57,000 members across half the state. Most places have something similar, whether a forum, an email list or a neighborhood association newsletter. *First step:* Ask one neighbor which email list or forum they read for local news, and sign up for it.
- **Join your local gift group and ask who is working on your issue** [Real now] Buy Nothing groups, which started on Bainbridge Island, Washington, in 2013, are drawn along neighborhood lines and have about 7.5 million members. People join them to give things away, but they are also one of the few places where many neighbors already read each other's posts. *First step:* Find your Buy Nothing group and post one line asking whether anyone knows who is working on your issue nearby.

### Climate change: “I'm worried about the planet heating up.”

Run `docs/research/possibilities/runs/climate/20261019-142001` · 14 cards · 19/20 examples verified

**1. Eating in a way that is easier on the climate**: *I still enjoy what I eat, and my meals put out a lot less carbon than they used to.*
- **Swap beef for chicken, beans or lentils a few nights a week** [Real now] A 2018 study of 38,000 farms in 119 countries found that a kilo of beef puts out about 60 kilos of greenhouse gases, while a kilo of peas puts out about one. You don’t have to give up beef to make a big difference; eating less of it does most of the work. *First step:* Pick two dinners this week where you'd normally have beef and plan a different protein.
- **Worry less about where food comes from than what it is** [Real now] The same study found that shipping is less than 10% of the emissions for most foods, and about half a percent for beef. Local beef still has a far bigger footprint than imported beans. *First step:* Look at your last grocery receipt and circle the three items with meat or dairy in them.

**2. Getting solar power without owning a roof**: *Part of the electricity I pay for comes from a solar farm nearby, and my bill is a little lower for it.*
- **Subscribe to a community solar farm** [Existing resource] In Minnesota, community solar gardens reached 939 megawatts in 2024, almost a third of the state’s solar, and subscribers’ bills are expected to drop 3% to 8%. Some people object that bills for non-subscribers rise 2% to 3%, which is why the program nearly ended in 2025. *First step:* Search your state name with "community solar" and see whether any program serves your utility.
- **Ask your state lawmakers to allow plug-in solar panels** [A change we’d make together] In 2025 Utah became the first state to let people plug solar systems of up to 1,200 watts straight into a home outlet, and the law passed with no votes against.
