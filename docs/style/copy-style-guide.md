# It copy style guide

**Status:** Draft for founder review (2026-10-17; revised the same day: clarity over brevity; plain titles, everyday American words, basics, ideas It could build). It applies to all It copy: pain, dream and fix text, page headings, status labels, the Support and Help pages, emails, and anything the generator writes. Where it conflicts with an older source document, this guide wins once the founder approves it.

## Who It sounds like

It sounds like the hometown doctor who has known you since you were a kid, or your closest mentor. That person:

- **knows you and talks to you, not at you.** They speak to one person, as an adult, without a script.
- **has seen a lot.** They can say "a school district in Seattle tried this", or "in Portugal it's now the law", because they've been paying attention. Specifics come out matter-of-factly, not as a sales pitch.
- **says exactly what they mean.** They'd never hand you a vague label like "Sleeping through" or "Both of us rested". They'd say "Falling asleep without dreading bed" or "Sleeping in separate rooms so we both rest". Clarity always beats brevity.
- **tells you the truth calmly.** That includes when something worked only once, costs money, or won't fit everyone. They don't hedge everything, and they don't oversell anything.
- **believes things can get better and doesn't need to shout it.** The hope comes from the example, not the adjectives.
- **never makes you feel judged.** They don't diagnose, moralize, or imply it's your fault. They offer ("One thing that's helped a lot of people…") rather than prescribe ("You should…").
- **can be a little wry about themselves**, never about your problem.

The founder's Support letter is the reference for this voice. A good test is whether a sentence could sit next to "If you can't, you're just as welcome here (I honestly couldn't right now myself)."

## What that rules out

These don't sound like that person, so avoid them:

- **Slogans and headlines.** "Boss can't text at 11." "Silent sky, 11 to 5." A mentor doesn't talk in taglines.
- **Staged moments and countdowns.** "At 11:01 the last jet has passed." "31 pauses an hour."
- **Contrast punchlines.** "…a breach, not a test of loyalty." "On purpose, not out of hope."
- **Fragments for effect.** Write full, natural sentences.
- **Titles that assume you know the example.** "Bring the Seattle results to your school board" only makes sense to someone who already knows about Seattle. The title says the action in plain terms ("Ask your school board for a later start time"), and the text underneath introduces the example.
- **Jargon, regional words, and abbreviations.** Use the everyday American word: "shift schedule", not "rota"; "doctor", not "GP"; "city council member", not "councillor"; "license", not "licence". Spell out acronyms the first time, or avoid them (say "insomnia therapy", not "CBT-I").
- **Roundabout phrasing.** "Join the people near you who are fighting night flights" becomes "Organize with neighbors to limit night flights". Say the action directly, with the fewest words that stay clear.
- **Euphemisms.** If an idea has a common name, use it. "Put a floor under income" hides "universal basic income", and readers can't tell what it means. Name it plainly and present it as a proposal people disagree about.
- **Passive dream lines.** "Something I care about making is real" has no one doing anything. Write "I finished the thing I wanted to make, and people are using it."
- **Short but empty.** Brevity is fine only when the words are specific. A short line or fix with no concrete detail reads as "vague and blah" (founder, 2026-10-19). Add the detail rather than trimming.
- **Vague labels.** A title that could sit under a different pain, or that doesn't say what actually changes, is too vague: "Rested", "Sleeping through", "Both of us rested", "Quiet evenings". Make it longer and clear instead.
- **Hype and self-help words**: journey, unlock, empower, transform, game-changer, level up, your best life, "you deserve". Also no exclamation marks.
- **Boilerplate caveats** in the copy: "AI proposal", "not verified", "results may vary", "no guarantee". Real status belongs in the status label (below). A limit worth knowing is said once, plainly, the way the mentor would say it (see *Being honest*).
- **Clinical or bureaucratic register**: "intervention", "stakeholders", "access pathways", "evidence-based modality".
- **Cheerleading** ("You've got this!") and **doom** ("Our democracy is collapsing").

## Being honest without hedging

- **Status labels carry the formal status.** Every idea shows one of: **Real now**, **Existing resource**, **Something It could build**, or **A change we'd make together**. Never describe something It could build as if it exists.
- **One plain limit, when it changes what you'd do.** "This was two schools in Seattle, so your district would be the real test." "It needs a doctor's referral in most areas." Say it once, in the same warm voice. Don't stack qualifiers. Usually zero or one "might" per item is enough.
- **Examples are stated as what happened**, with who, where and roughly when: "In 2016 Seattle moved high school start times to 8:45, and teens slept about half an hour more." No invented precision and no rounding up.
- **Contested ideas are offered as ideas.** "Some people want…", "Sleep doctors are pushing for…". Name who holds the view, and leave room for people who'd choose differently.

## The pieces

| Piece | What it is | Guidance | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Pain line** | What someone would say out loud | First person, everyday words, one issue. No diagnosis. | I don't sleep well. |
| **Dream title** | A better future, named the way a person would describe their own life | Clear before short. Say what is different, in plain words, usually 3–8. Test: could this title belong to another pain, or would someone ask "meaning what?" If so, it's too vague. Not a slogan. | Evenings free from work messages |
| **Dream line** | What that future is like, for me | First person, one sentence, concrete enough to picture without staging a scene. | When I finish work, I'm actually finished. |
| **Fix title** | Something someone can do | Plain imperative, saying who does what. It must make sense to someone who has never heard of the example; names of examples go in the fix text, not the title. | Ask your school board for a later start time |
| **Fix text** | What it is and why it might help *you* | 1–3 sentences: the idea, the real example said plainly, and any one limit worth knowing. | Portugal made it the law in 2022: outside working hours, employers aren't supposed to contact staff except in a real emergency. Your team doesn't need a law to agree on the same thing. |
| **First step** | The smallest real action | One sentence, starts with a verb, doable in 20 minutes. | Suggest a simple rule, like no work messages between 8pm and 8am, and see who's in. |
| **Status label** | Formal honesty | Use one of the four fixed labels above. | Real now |

Never shorten a title or line in a way that loses clarity. If the clear version is longer, use the longer one.

There is **no separate "scene" line**. Concreteness lives in the dream line and the real example.

## Kinds of fixes

Every fix is one of these kinds. The app shows each kind with an icon and a word, so the copy doesn't have to explain it.

| Kind | Icon (Lucide) | What it is | How to write it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Feature** | `hammer` | Something It would build (status: Something It could build) | A short feature pitch, verb first: "Shows employee-owned workplaces near you, from the public worker co-op directory and federal employee-stock-plan data, so you have somewhere to apply or a model to point to." The title names the capability ("Shared signup for covering town meetings"), not a user action. Never "It could" or "with It". |
| **Task** | `circle-dot-dashed` | Something you do once, including one-time changes that keep working afterward (grayscale, blackout curtains, one charging spot) | Name the action and its object. "Ask your school board for a later start time." |
| **Schedule** | `calendar-sync` | A recurring commitment on the calendar | Say what repeats and how often. "Set up a weekly call or walk with one friend." |
| **Habit** | `repeat` | A behavior triggered by a cue, repeated without planning (use sparingly) | Name the cue and the behavior. "When you meet someone new on the street, note their name." |
| **Practice** | `sprout` | A skill you get better at over time, usually in conversation | Name the skill. Every Practice carries a `featureIdea`: a one-line, verb-first coaching feature It could build ("Helps you prepare for a hard conversation and checks in afterward"). The app shows it on hover. |
| **Learn** | `graduation-cap` | A skill or piece of knowledge you can use yourself, often in one sitting | Name what you'll be able to do. "Learn box breathing to calm down in a minute." "Know your rights before an eviction hearing." Learning about someone else's example ("Learn how one city…") is not a Learn; turn it into what you do. |
| **Project** | `folder-kanban` | A bounded effort over days or weeks, with several steps and an end | Name the finished thing. "Organize a block party with your neighbors." |
| **Group** | `users` | Join an existing group | Name the group. "Join your building's tenant association." |
| **New group** | `user-round-plus` | Start a group that doesn't exist yet | Name what you'd start. "Start a tenant association in your building." |
| **Path** | `footprints` | A sequence of the above toward a bigger change (later) | Not used yet. |

Rules for every kind:
- **One idea per fix.** If a fix combines two actions, split it.
- **Learning is fine when you can use it.** A Learn fix teaches a technique or knowledge the reader will use (box breathing, reading a lease, knowing your rights). A fix that only says "Learn how one city…" or "Read about…" someone else's example isn't an action. Put the example in the text and make the fix what the person does with it. Not "Learn how one city became a large landlord", but "Ask candidates whether your city should build and own homes like Vienna does."
- **Questions It should ask.** When a fix is really a question the person should answer about their situation ("Is the problem the work or how it's managed?"), it belongs to It as a Feature (a follow-up question), not as a Task for the person.

Feature pitches are honest because the Feature tag and the "Something It could build" status are always shown with them. The text describes the intended behavior and what data or tools would make it work. Feature titles say what you'd get or be able to do ("See employee-owned workplaces near you"). A feature's first step is something you can do today without It, or "Tell us you'd use this".

## Basics belong too

Obvious, practical fixes (blackout curtains, a white noise machine, earplugs) are often what helps most. Include them. Make them useful with one concrete detail rather than leaving them out for being unoriginal. "Turn the obvious into something more meaningful" can be as simple as saying *why* it works or *which kind* to get.

## Mechanics

- American English, with contractions ("don't", "it's"). Spell out numbers one to nine except times, money, and statistics.
- "Support", never "donate" or "donation". It is not a charity.
- "We Are It" is the full name of both the organization and the app; "It" is the short name (see `GLOSSARY.md`). Use the full name where it reads as a formal party, as in the signup line "By continuing, you acknowledge We Are It's Privacy Policy".
- "It" (capital I) is the product, so avoid starting a sentence with the pronoun "it". Readers can't tell "It's common" or "It isn't right for everyone" from the product. Name the thing instead ("Sleeping apart is common", "This therapy isn't right for everyone").
- In running text, "we" means the people involved in It, which is not yet a crowd. Don't imply scale It doesn't have.
- Plain but specific: short common words, and real names and numbers where they help. There is no reading-level formula, but if a 12-year-old would stumble on a word, find another.
- Sentence case for titles and headings.
- Use the Oxford (serial) comma in every list: "forms, bills, and calls", "rivers, lakes, or drinking water". The pain and possibility lints flag a list without it (founder, 2026-10-20).

## Before and after (Sleep)

**Before:** *Boss can't text at 11*. "My evenings belong to me." Fix: "Write Portugal's no-contact rule into your team agreement. After-hours messages become a breach, not a test of loyalty."
**After (round 2):** *Evenings free from work messages*. "When I finish work, I'm actually finished, and my phone stays quiet until morning." Fix: **Agree on quiet hours with your team**. "Portugal made it the law in 2022: outside working hours, employers aren't supposed to contact staff except in a real emergency. Your team doesn't need a law to agree on the same thing." First step: "Suggest a simple rule, like no work messages between 8pm and 8am, and see who's in."

**Before:** *Asleep in six weeks*. "I fall asleep without pills or dread." Fix: "Four short sessions reset your sleep window instead of another prescription."
**After:** *Falling asleep without pills or dread*. "I go to bed expecting to sleep, and most nights I do." Fix: **Ask your doctor about short, drug-free insomnia therapy**. "In a large trial in England's health service, four short sessions with a practice nurse helped people with insomnia more than the usual advice leaflet, without pills. It isn't right for everyone, so the nurse checks first." First step: "Jot down when you went to bed and got up for the next week, so you have something to bring."

**Before:** *Silent sky, 11 to 5*. "The planes stop when I sleep."
**After:** *No planes over my house at night*. "Nothing flies overhead between when I go to bed and when I get up." Fix: **Organize with neighbors to limit night flights**. "In Frankfurt, neighbors took the airport to court and won a ban on flights from 11pm to 5am." First step: "Look up whether there's a noise group for your nearest airport, and read their latest update."

**Before:** *Two beds, one marriage*. "We sleep apart and wake up closer."
**After:** *Sleeping in separate rooms so we both rest*. "My partner and I both sleep through the night, even if that means separate rooms some nights." Fix: **Try sleeping apart for two weeks**. "More than a third of American adults sleep in a different room from their partner at least sometimes. It's common, and it doesn't have to mean anything is wrong." First step: "Pick a start date together and decide who gets which room."
